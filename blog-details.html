The Architecture of a Tasting Menu

A composed menu works like a room: entrance, tension, release, memory.

  • By Rafael Ortiz
  • 6 min read
  • Kitchen notes
Kitchen
Rafael Moreau 28 Jul 2026 7 min

A composed menu works like a room: entrance, tension, release, and memory. Get the sequence wrong and even flawless cooking lands flat.

Most guests describe a tasting menu by its best dish. We plan it by its transitions. The fourth course is rarely anyone's favourite, but if it fails, the fifth one cannot recover, and the table starts checking the time.

The entrance

The first two courses exist to reset the palate and lower the volume of the room. They are bright, small, and cold or barely warm. Nothing rich arrives before a guest has taken their coat off and had a proper look at the space.

The tension

Courses three through six build weight, temperature, and aromatic pressure. This is where fire and fermentation do the work. Each plate should feel like it costs the kitchen something, and the pacing tightens deliberately as the room fills.

The release

After the heaviest course we drop back hard. A clear broth, an acidic cut, or a raw preparation. Guests read it as generosity, but it is structural: without it the dessert cannot land, and the last hour drags.

The memory

The final two plates and the pour that follows are what people describe a week later. That is why pastry sits under Anika rather than the savoury brigade, and why the last course is the one we rewrite most often.

The rule underneath all of it: if a course cannot be executed identically at 10 PM on a Saturday with a full room, it does not belong on the menu, however well it tastes in an empty kitchen at three in the afternoon.

Key takeaways

Plan transitions, not highlights.

Drop the intensity before dessert.

Cut anything you cannot repeat under pressure.

The Sequence in Practice

The current menu, mapped to the argument above.

Course Role Weight Served At
1 - 2 Entrance Light, cold, acidic Within 12 minutes of seating
3 - 4 Building tension Warm, fat entering 25 - 45 minutes
5 - 6 Peak Fire, reduction, smoke 50 - 80 minutes
7 Release Clear, sharp, raw 90 minutes
8 - 9 Memory Sweet, floral, warm 105 - 130 minutes
10 Close A final pour At the guest's pace
Reader Notes

Replies worth publishing.

From a chef

"The fourth course point is right."

"We spent two years fixing our sixth course when the problem was always the fourth. Reading this cost me an evening of rewriting our menu, so thank you, I suppose."

From a guest

"So that is why the broth arrives."

"I always thought the clear course was the kitchen taking a breather. Turns out it was doing the most work of the night."

From the floor

"Pacing is a service problem too."

"Worth adding that the room reads the transitions as much as the plates. If we clear too fast, the release course lands as an interruption."
